Extinction risk with a zoospore pool (Wilber 2017)

Wilber, Knapp, Toothman and Briggs (2017, Ecology Letters 20:1169–1181, 10.1111/ele.12814) extend the host–parasite integral projection model of Wilber et al. (2016) to ask whether extinction of Rana muscosa populations depends more on transmission or on host resistance and tolerance. They add an environmental zoospore pool fed by infected adults and tadpoles, three tadpole stages, a seasonal temperature cycle, a yearly demographic update with density-dependent recruitment, and demographic stochasticity. The implementation here was checked against the paper, the authors' code (github.com/mqwilber/host_extinction; their March 2017 preprint and appendix in that repository stand in for Appendices S1–S4, which returned HTTP 403) and their data (Dryad 10.5061/dryad.p614h, CC0, bundled).

Additions to the IPM machinery

Two generic pieces are added to the IPM machinery:

  • R0 when infected hosts also shed into an environmental pool. The pool is one more infected state, so the next-generation matrix of Klepac and Caswell (2011) has rank one and R0 = [t; β]ᵀ (I − U)⁻¹ [s0 G0; 0] (Appendix S2): the host term plus a pool term.
  • One step of a matrix model with demographic stochasticity (Caswell 2001; Schreiber and Ross 2016): individuals move by multinomial draws on the columns of the transition matrix, with death as the remainder, and births or shed pathogens are Poisson.
Chytridiomycosis.reservoir_reproduction_number — Function
reservoir_reproduction_number(d::DiscretizedIPM; host_transmission, pool_transmission,
                              shedding, pool_survival) -> NamedTuple

R0 of a discretised host–parasite IPM whose infected hosts also shed into an environmental pool Z that infects susceptible hosts (Wilber et al. 2017, Appendix S2; Klepac & Caswell 2011). The infected states are the n load bins and the pool:

U = [ P   0 ]        M = [ s0 G0 ] [ tᵀ  β ]
    [ fᵀ  ν ]            [  0    ]

with P the infected kernel, f (shedding, a scalar or one entry per bin) the pathogen added to the pool per infected host per step, ν (pool_survival) the survival of the pool per step, t (host_transmission, scalar or per bin) the number of hosts infected per step by one infected host and β (pool_transmission) the number infected per step per unit of pool, both in a fully susceptible population and linearised at the disease-free state. R0 = ρ(M (I − U)⁻¹) = [t; β]ᵀ (I − U)⁻¹ [s0 G0; 0] because M has rank one.

Returns total (R0 with the pool), host (R0 without it, β = 0; the method of basic_reproduction_number for a DiscretizedIPM) and environment (total − host, the pool's contribution, β fᵀ(I − P)⁻¹ s0 G0 / (1 − ν)).

Chytridiomycosis.demographic_projection — Function
demographic_projection(rng, T, F, n) -> Vector{Float64}

One step of a matrix population model with demographic stochasticity (Caswell 2001, ch. 15; Schreiber & Ross 2016), for integer numbers n of individuals in each state:

  • the nⱼ individuals of state j move independently: a multinomial draw over the states with probabilities T[:, j], the remainder 1 − Σᵢ T[i, j] dying (negative entries, and a column sum above 1 from rounding, are clipped and the column renormalised, as Wilber et al. 2017 do);
  • each state j produces Poisson(nⱼ F[i, j]) new individuals of state i (births, or parasites shed into a pool).

T + F is the mean projection matrix. demographic_projection! writes into an existing vector.

The hybrid model

The state is u = [T1, T2, T3, S, n_1, …, n_m, Z]: tadpoles of years 1–3, uninfected adults, infected adults per load bin (30 bins on [−5, 18] ln ZE per swab) and zoospores. Every 3-day step (eqns 1–3; Appendix S3):

\[\begin{aligned} T_i(t+1) &= s_{T_i} T_i(t), \qquad S(t+1) = s_0 (1-\phi) S + \textstyle\sum_x s(x)\, l(x,T)\, n(x), \\ n(x',t+1) &= \textstyle\sum_x s(x)(1-l(x,T)) G(x',x,T)\, n(x) + s_0 \phi S\, G_0(x',T), \\ Z(t+1) &= \nu(T) Z + \mu_A \textstyle\sum_x e^x n(x) + \mu_T \sum_i T_i - \psi , \end{aligned}\]

with φ = 1 − exp(−Λ Δt) from one of the transmission functions of Table 2 and the zoospore survival ν(T) from Woodhams et al. (2008). Temperature follows a cosine from 4 °C in winter to 20 °C in summer (period 366 days). Once a year, in the step at which the rising temperature reaches 11.79 °C, adults reproduce (pA λ year-1 tadpoles per surviving adult), year-1 and year-2 tadpoles age or metamorphose, and metamorphs join the uninfected adults with Ricker survival exp(−A/K). With demographic stochasticity, every frog and zoospore moves by a multinomial draw on the discretised kernel, and tadpoles and shed zoospores are Poisson. The stochastic simulator draws from a seeded rng, like DrawertIBMProblem; the deterministic map lowers to a SciMLBase.DiscreteProblem.

Chytridiomycosis.zoospore_survival — Function
zoospore_survival(p::Wilber2017Parameters, temperature) -> Float64

Probability ν(T) that a zoospore in the pool survives one step of p.Δt days at temperature (°C): exp(−d(T) Δt), with d(T) the death rate (per day) interpolated by a natural cubic spline through the four rates of Woodhams et al. (2008, Table 1; compendium woodhams2008, sets table1_*), as the authors' get_zoospore_surv_prob does with R's smooth.spline (which on four points selects the interpolating spline). Linear extrapolation outside 4–28 °C. If p.ν is a number it is returned instead.

At 4, 12 and 20 °C, ν is 0.823, 0.804 and 0.565 per 3 days.

Chytridiomycosis.HybridIPMProblem — Type
HybridIPMProblem(p::Wilber2017Parameters; mesh = IPMMesh(:wilber2017), years = 25,
                 temperature = nothing, u0 = nothing, stochastic = true,
                 zoospore_removal = true)

The hybrid model of Wilber et al. (2017, Fig. 1; Appendix S3): the within-season IPM of adult load with three tadpole stages and a zoospore pool, stepped every p.Δt = 3 days, and the yearly demographic update. The state is u = [T1, T2, T3, S, n_1, …, n_m, Z] (numbers per mesh bin for infected adults).

  • mesh: the load mesh, 30 bins on [−5, 18] ln ZE by default (compendium).
  • years: length in years of days_per_year = 366 days (122 steps; compendium).
  • temperature: °C as a number or a function of time in days; by default the authors' cosine, T(k) = 12 − 8 cos(2π k/122 + acos((T_start − 12)/−8)) at step k, from 4 °C in winter to 20 °C in summer (SeasonalTemperature).
  • u0: initial state; by default 10 uninfected adults, 85, 12 and 3 tadpoles and no zoospores (compendium).
  • stochastic = true: demographic stochasticity (Appendix S4): multinomial transitions of individuals and zoospores, Poisson births and shedding (demographic_projection). false iterates the mean map.
  • zoospore_removal = true: the authors' code removes the zoospores taken up by newly infected frogs (ψ of eqn 3); false drops the term as the text describes.

The step to time k uses the temperature at time k and the infection probability of the state at k − 1; the yearly update happens in the step at which round(T, 2) first equals T_repro = 11.79 °C in spring (step 30 of each year), as in the authors' code. solve(prob; rng) returns a HybridIPMSolution; extinction_curve repeats stochastic runs.

Chytridiomycosis.basic_reproduction_number — Method
basic_reproduction_number(p::Wilber2017Parameters; temperature, S,
                          mesh = IPMMesh(:wilber2017; n = 50), as_coded = true)
    -> NamedTuple

R0 of Bd invading S susceptible adults at a constant temperature (°C), with and without the environmental zoospore pool (Fig. 2; Appendix S2): reservoir_reproduction_number of the within-season IPM, linearising Λ at the disease-free state (ln(Z + 1) ≈ Z). Chytridiomycosis.stasis_load — Function
stasis_load(p::Wilber2017Parameters; temperature) -> NamedTuple

The graphical argument of Fig. 4: the load at which the mean growth function crosses the 1:1 line, x* = (b01 + b21 T)/(1 − b11) (ln ZE per swab; resistance), and the probability s(x*) of surviving a step there (tolerance). Because s is steep near x*, small changes in b11 or b00 move s(x*) a lot. Returns x and survival; x = Inf and survival s(∞) when b11 ≥ 1 (loads grow without bound).

Chytridiomycosis.extinction_sensitivity — Function
extinction_sensitivity(p::Wilber2017Parameters; nsims = 1000, σ = 0.3, years = 8,
                       mesh = IPMMesh(:wilber2017), rng = Random.default_rng(),
                       zoospore_removal = true) -> NamedTuple

The global sensitivity analysis of Fig. 3 (Methods; sensitivity_analysis.R, after Sobie 2009): in each of nsims runs every transmission, resistance and tolerance parameter is multiplied by an independent lognormal factor with median 1 and sdlog = σ, and one stochastic run of HybridIPMProblem records whether the population went extinct within years. The parameters are b00, b10 (tolerance: survival), b01, b21, b11, v01, c01, b03, b13, v03, c03, b02, b22, b12 (resistance: growth, initial load, loss) and β0, β1 (transmission; β1 only for density or frequency dependence): 16 for :zpool_dd and :zpool_fd, 15 for :zpool.

Returns names, the perturbed values X (nsims × length(names)) and extinct (Bool per run). sensitivity_weights fits the logistic regression of Fig. 3.

source
Chytridiomycosis.sensitivity_weights — Function
sensitivity_weights(X, y; C = 1.0) -> NamedTuple

The regularised logistic regression of Fig. 3: columns of X are z-scored (population standard deviation, as scikit-learn's StandardScaler) and extinction y is regressed on them by L2-penalised logistic regression with inverse penalty C, minimising wᵀw/2 + C Σ log(1 + exp(−yᵢ wᵀxᵢ)) with yᵢ = ±1 and the intercept as an extra, penalised feature (scikit-learn's default liblinear solver of 2017). Returns weights (one per column; positive means the parameter raises the extinction probability) and intercept.

Printed and coded details that differ

  • Table 1 mixes two fits. The survival row is a Bayesian posterior mean on the z-scored load (whose mean 7.186 and sd 2.961 ln ZE are not printed). The growth, loss and initial-load rows are the Wilber (2016) REML and ML fits, but the simulations used Bayesian posterior means (bayesian_parameter_estimates.rds): for example b11 0.796 rather than 0.799, v01 5.84 rather than 5.92 and v03 0.71 rather than 0.59.
  • R0 (Fig. 2). The figure is the median over 500 posterior draws, which is lower than R0 at the point estimates. The authors' R0 code omits Δt (it uses β per day as a probability per step), sheds ln(eˣ + 1) zoospores per infected adult (Appendix S2 says f x; eqn 3 and the simulation use eˣ) and sets s0 = 1. as_coded = false gives R0 of the model as simulated, which is far larger.
  • Zoospore removal. The text says the uptake of zoospores by newly infected frogs (ψ) "had very little effect … we do not consider it further"; the code includes it (zoospore_removal = true).
  • Births. Appendix S4 says Poisson births with mean A pA λ; the code uses pA λ times the adults that survive the step.
  • Transmission priors. The latent-pool fits give zoospores a death rate with prior Normal(0.3, 0.03) per day "from Woodhams et al. 2008"; the simulation uses Woodhams's measured rates, 0.065–0.19 per day over 4–20 °C.
  • Loss-of-infection priors. The Table 1 footnote gives Normal(0, 5) priors to all b; the Stan model of l(x) has its priors commented out (flat).
  • The code's extinction simulations use 25 years (the YAML file says 20).

How it relates to Wilber 2016 and the Briggs model

The within-season kernel is the 2016 IPM with the survival function refitted on the z-scored load (on the raw scale, intercept 11.59 and slope −0.876, close to the 2016 deposit) and environmental infection replaced by the zoospore pool. Like the Briggs et al. (2010) model, it has tadpoles that are always infected and feed a pool, and density-dependent recruitment; it differs in using fitted load regressions instead of sporangial growth to a lethal threshold. The pool makes transmission nearly independent of host density, which is why the three transmission functions give similar extinction curves.
