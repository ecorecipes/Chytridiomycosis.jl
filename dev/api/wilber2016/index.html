Integral projection models (Wilber 2016)

An integral projection model (IPM) follows the distribution of a continuous trait through discrete time. In a host–parasite IPM the trait is pathogen load, so the model sits between an individual-based load model such as Briggs et al. (2010) or Drawert et al. (2017), which follows every host, and a compartmental model, which follows only how many hosts are infected. This page has two parts: generic IPM machinery, and the reference implementation of Wilber, Langwig, Kilpatrick, McCallum and Briggs (2016, Methods Ecol Evol 7:1182–1194, 10.1111/2041-210X.12561), checked against the paper, the authors' R code and results (github.com/mqwilber/ipmforparasites) and their data (Dryad 10.5061/dryad.06sd9, CC0, bundled).

Generic machinery

The state is one uninfected class S and the infected density I(x) over load x (eqns 1–2 without reproduction):

\[S(t+1) = s_0(1-\phi) S(t) + \int s(x)\,l(x)\,I(x,t)\,dx, \qquad I(x',t+1) = \int s(x)\,(1-l(x))\,G(x',x)\,I(x,t)\,dx + s_0\,\phi\,S(t)\,G_0(x').\]

An IPMKernel holds the vital-rate functions: survival s(x), growth G(x′, x) (a distribution for each x), loss of infection l(x), initial load G0(x′) and uninfected survival s0. discretize evaluates it on an IPMMesh by the midpoint rule (or exact bin integrals, integration = :cdf). Hosts whose next load falls outside the mesh are lost (eviction = :none, as in the paper) or kept in the edge bins (eviction = :boundary). eviction measures the loss and mesh_convergence checks the mesh size. The infection probability φ is passed to projection_matrix separately, because it may depend on the state.

The matrix analyses work on any projection matrix: population_growth_rate (the dominant eigenvalue λ), stable_distribution, sensitivity_matrix and elasticity_matrix, and absorption_times (mean and variance of the time to death, from the fundamental matrix). basic_reproduction_number has a method for a DiscretizedIPM: R0 by the next-generation method of Klepac and Caswell (2011).

A midpoint-rule mesh of n equal bins on [lower, upper] (Easterling et al. 2000; Rees et al. 2014). meshpoints gives the bin midpoints, meshwidth the bin width h = (upper − lower)/n and meshedges the n + 1 bin edges.

Chytridiomycosis.IPMKernel — Type
IPMKernel(; survival, growth, loss = x -> 0.0, initial_load, uninfected_survival = 1.0)

The vital-rate functions of a host–parasite IPM with an uninfected class (Wilber et al. 2016, eqns 1–2 and Fig. 1), on whatever load scale the mesh uses:

  • survival(x): probability that an infected host with load x survives a time step, s(x);
  • growth(x): the distribution (a Distributions.UnivariateDistribution) of the load at t + 1 of a host that survives with load x and keeps its infection, G(x′, x);
  • loss(x): probability that a surviving host with load x loses its infection and returns to the uninfected class, l(x);
  • initial_load: the distribution of load on first infection, G0(x′);
  • uninfected_survival: survival of uninfected hosts, s0.

Infected hosts survive, then lose their infection or not, then grow: the infected kernel is s(x)(1 − l(x))G(x′, x) and the return to the uninfected class is s(x) l(x). The infection probability φ is not part of the kernel; it is given to projection_matrix or computed from the state. Use discretize to turn a kernel into matrices.

IPMKernel(p::WilberParameters; temperature)

The kernel of Wilber et al. (2016) at temperature (°C): survival s(x), normal growth G(x′, x, T), loss of infection l(x, T), normal initial load G0(x′, T) and s0 (see WilberParameters).

source
Chytridiomycosis.DiscretizedIPM — Type
DiscretizedIPM

A host–parasite IPM discretised on a mesh by discretize. Fields:

  • mesh: the IPMMesh;
  • P: the n × n infected-to-infected matrix, P[i, j] the expected number of hosts in bin i at t + 1 per host in bin j at t (columns are "from");
  • loss: the vector s(x_j) l(x_j), infected hosts in bin j returning to the uninfected class;
  • recruitment: the distribution over bins of the load of a newly infected host (h G0(x_i) with the midpoint rule);
  • uninfected_survival: s0.

projection_matrix assembles the full (n + 1) × (n + 1) matrix for a given infection probability, with the uninfected class first.

source
Chytridiomycosis.discretize — Function
discretize(k::IPMKernel, mesh::IPMMesh; integration = :midpoint, eviction = :none)
    -> DiscretizedIPM

Discretise the kernel on mesh.

  • integration = :midpoint (default) evaluates the growth and initial-load densities at bin midpoints and multiplies by the bin width, as Wilber et al. (2016) do. :cdf integrates each density exactly over each bin.
  • eviction = :none (default) lets hosts whose next load falls outside the mesh leave the model, as in Wilber et al. (2016), who chose wide bounds instead. :boundary returns that mass to the first or last bin (a floor and ceiling; Williams, Miller & Ellner 2012).

eviction measures how much is lost; mesh_convergence checks the mesh size.

source
Chytridiomycosis.projection_matrix — Function
projection_matrix(d::DiscretizedIPM, φ) -> Matrix{Float64}

The (n + 1) × (n + 1) projection matrix for infection probability φ per time step, with the uninfected class first (Wilber et al. 2016, "Analysing the IPM"; Merow et al. 2014):

A = [ s0(1 − φ)        s(x)ᵀ l(x)ᵀ
      s0 φ G0           P            ]

Chytridiomycosis.absorption_times — Function
absorption_times(A::AbstractMatrix) -> (mean, variance)

Mean and variance of the number of time steps before absorption (death, and eviction from the mesh), starting from each state of the transient matrix A: with the fundamental matrix N = (I − A)⁻¹, mean = 1ᵀN and variance = 1ᵀ(2N² − N) − mean² (Caswell 2001; the authors' absorption_times). Multiply by the time step for a duration.

source
Chytridiomycosis.eviction — Function
eviction(k::IPMKernel, mesh::IPMMesh) -> (growth, initial)

How much of each density falls outside the mesh (Williams, Miller & Ellner 2012): growth is the probability, for a host at each midpoint, that its next load lies outside [lower, upper]; initial is the same for the initial-load distribution. The midpoint rule with eviction = :none loses these hosts.

source
Chytridiomycosis.mesh_convergence — Function
mesh_convergence(f, sizes; rtol = 1e-3) -> (sizes, values, relative_change, converged)

Evaluate a scalar summary f(n) of an IPM discretised with n bins for each n in sizes (increasing), and report the relative change between successive sizes. converged is true when the last change is below rtol. For example mesh_convergence(n -> population_growth_rate(p; temperature = 20, mesh = IPMMesh(-5, 18, n)), [25, 50, 100, 200]).

load_summary(mesh::IPMMesh, u::AbstractVector) -> NamedTuple

Summaries of a host–parasite state u = [S; n_1, …, n_m] (numbers of hosts): total, uninfected, infected, prevalence, and the mean, variance and variance-to-mean ratio vm_ratio of load among infected hosts, on the mesh's scale (Wilber et al. 2016, Fig. 5 uses natural-log loads).

The Bd–Rana muscosa model

Twenty adult frogs were exposed to about 10⁶ Bd zoospores, housed singly at 4, 12, 20 and 26 °C (five per temperature; 26 °C was not analysed) and swabbed every 3 days. One time step is 3 days. Load x is the natural log of zoospore equivalents (ZE) per swab; uninfected is a separate class, not x = 0. The vital rates (Table 1) are regressions on x and temperature T:

  • survival: logit s(x) = b00 + b10 x, fitted at 20 °C (no frog died at 4 or 12 °C);
  • growth: normal with mean b01 + b11 x + b21 T and variance v01 exp(2 c01 x);
  • loss of infection: logit l(x, T) = b02 + b12 x + b22 T;
  • initial load: normal with mean b03 + b13 T and variance v03 exp(2 c03 T);
  • infection: logit φ(T) = b04 + b14 T; frogs were housed singly, so this is infection from the environment.

The kernel is discretised by the midpoint rule with 100 bins on [−5, 18].

Chytridiomycosis.load_growth_rate — Method
load_growth_rate(p::WilberParameters; temperature, x = 0.0) -> Float64

Expected change of ln load per day of an infected frog that survives and stays infected, (b01 + (b11 − 1) x + b21 T)/Δt, at load x (ln ZE per swab) and temperature (°C). Unlike the exponential growth of the Briggs et al. (2010) model (load_growth_rate(::BriggsParameters, ::Any)), the fitted growth function (b11 < 1) pulls loads towards x* = (b01 + b21 T)/(1 − b11), where the rate is zero.

Chytridiomycosis.BdIPMProblem — Type
BdIPMProblem(p::WilberParameters; temperature, mesh = IPMMesh(:wilber2016),
             S0 = 100, I0 = zeros(mesh.n), days = 120, transmission = nothing,
             integration = :midpoint, eviction = :none)

The Wilber et al. (2016) host–parasite IPM projected for days (in steps of p.Δt = 3 days) from S0 uninfected frogs and infected frogs I0 (numbers per mesh bin).

  • temperature: °C, a number or a function of time in days (the kernel is then rebuilt each step, for seasonal runs).
  • transmission = nothing: the infection probability is φ(T) of the regression (eqns 7–8, density-independent). A LoadTransmission makes it depend on the state (eqn 13).
  • integration, eviction: passed to discretize.

S0 and days default to the compendium values of the paper's summer simulations (100 frogs, 120 days). solve(prob) iterates the map directly and returns a BdIPMSolution; to_discrete_problem gives the same map as a SciMLBase.DiscreteProblem.

Chytridiomycosis.LoadTransmission — Type
LoadTransmission(ω, β; as_coded = false)

Density-dependent transmission with an environmental reservoir (Wilber et al. 2016, eqn 13): the probability that an uninfected host is infected in one step is

φ = 1 − exp(−(a + β Z)),   a = −ln(1 − ω),

where ω is the probability of infection from the environment alone and Z is the pathogen in the host population.

  • as_coded = false (default): Z = ∫ x I(x, t) dx, as printed, the sum of the (log) loads of infected hosts.
  • as_coded = true: Z = ln(Σ eˣ n(x) + 1), the log of the total zoospore load plus one, as in the authors' density_dependent_zoospores. This is what produced the paper's density-dependent results (Figs S7–S8).

Use it as the transmission of a BdIPMProblem; infection_probability(tr, mesh, u) evaluates it for a state u = [S; n].

source

infection_probability has methods for WilberParameters (φ(T)) and for a LoadTransmission and a state.

Chytridiomycosis.population_growth_rate — Method
population_growth_rate(p::WilberParameters; temperature, mesh = IPMMesh(:wilber2016),
                       integration = :midpoint, eviction = :none) -> Float64

The population growth rate λ per 3-day step of the density-independent IPM (eqns 7–8; Fig. 4b) at temperature (°C): the dominant eigenvalue of the full matrix with the uninfected class. There is no reproduction, so λ ≤ 1 and λ^(120/3) is the fraction surviving a 120-day summer in the long run.

Chytridiomycosis.vital_rate_elasticities — Function
vital_rate_elasticities(p::WilberParameters; temperature, δ = 0.001,
                        mesh = IPMMesh(:wilber2016), of = :λ) -> NamedTuple

Elasticities of the population growth rate (of = :λ) or of the variance-to-mean ratio of the stable load distribution (of = :vm_ratio) to each regression coefficient (Wilber et al. 2016, 'Analysing the IPM', Figs S5–S6): e_b = (f(b(1 + δ)) − f(b)) / (δ f(b)), keyed by coefficient (b00, b10, …, s0). s0 = 1 is perturbed downwards (s0(1 − δ)), since a survival probability cannot exceed 1. The values are signed; the authors plot absolute values.

Chytridiomycosis.fit_wilber2016 — Function
fit_wilber2016(rows = load_wilber2016(); survival_temperatures = (20,)) -> WilberFit

Refit the five vital-rate regressions of Wilber et al. (2016, Table 1) to the deposited data, following the authors' R script (see the comments in src/fitting/wilber2016.jl): logistic regressions for survival, loss of infection and infection, and generalized least squares with an exponential variance function (REML) for growth and initial load.

WilberFit

Chytridiomycosis.growth_rate_bootstrap — Function
growth_rate_bootstrap(fit::WilberFit; temperatures = 4:20, n = 1000,
                      mesh = IPMMesh(:wilber2016; n = 50), rng = Random.default_rng(),
                      as_coded = false) -> Matrix{Float64}

The parametric bootstrap of the population growth rate of Wilber et al. (2016, Fig. 4b): n draws of the regression coefficients of each vital-rate function from a multivariate normal with the fitted covariance (variance parameters v, c and s0 held fixed), and λ of the IPM at each temperature for each draw. What reproduces

Paper and deposit are compared in test/wilber2016.jl and the validation targets wilber2016_*. "Deposit" means the authors' own result files, decoded from the vector graphics of their results/*.pdf (to about 10⁻⁵) or read from their CSVs.

  • Vital-rate fits. Every coefficient and covariance of the deposited fit is reproduced by fit_wilber2016 to at least 8 significant figures, as are the growth, loss, initial-load and infection tests of the Results (χ² = 196.36, 13.56, 9.8, 14.555, 23.701, 6.00, 6.0361; t₄₁ = 2.53).
  • Survival. Table 1's survival coefficients (11.824, −0.8605) and the survival test (χ² = 12.197) are those of a fit to all three temperatures, not the 20 °C fit the text and Fig. 2a describe. The 20 °C fit gives 11.597, −0.8873 and χ² = 8.58, and is what the authors' analyses used. WilberParameters(:wilber2016) is Table 1 as printed; set = "deposited" is what the figures were made with.
  • Density-independent IPM (deposited coefficients, 100 bins): λ for 4–20 °C (0.99896 to 0.93690), the mean and variance-to-mean ratio of the stable load distribution (2.61 at 4 °C to 0.85 at 20 °C, Fig. 5 inset), the peaks of Fig. 5 and the mean times to death of Fig. 4a all agree with the deposit to plotting precision.
  • R0 (Appendix S1 code, 50 bins): full and collapsed R0 agree with the deposited plot to 4 × 10⁻⁵; R0 = 1 near 12.2 °C and peaks at 1.68 near 17 °C.
  • Density-dependent transmission (eqn 13 as coded, 40 × 40 grid, 12–20 °C): the population after 120 days and the fraction uninfected agree with the deposited CSVs to 10⁻¹¹.
  • Bootstrap (Fig. 4b): medians agree within sampling error; the very wide interquartile ranges come from a slip in the authors' code, which never resamples the survival slope (as_coded = true). With both survival coefficients resampled the ranges are much narrower.

Printed statements that do not hold, or hold only for the code:

  • Eqn 13 integrates x I(x, t) (log loads); the code uses ln(Σ eˣ n + 1), the log of the total zoospore load. The conclusion that density-dependent transmission "had little effect" holds as coded; with eqn 13 as printed, β matters a lot when environmental infection is rare (at 20 °C and ω = 0.01, the summer decline rises from 16% to 79% across the β range, against 16% to 32% as coded).
  • "Populations at 12 °C will experience a maximum of a 20% population decline … with 70% prevalence": the deposited simulation gives at most 25% and 77%.
  • The text describes 20 frogs at three temperatures, five per temperature; the data have five frogs at each of four temperatures, and 26 °C is silently excluded.
  • The paper scans ω over 0.01–0.6; the deposited simulation stops at 0.3.

How it relates to the Briggs and Drawert models

All three describe Bd load on R. muscosa/R. sierrae. Briggs et al. (2010) and Drawert et al. (2017) grow sporangia exponentially (rate ηνf − σ per day, load_growth_rate) until a hard lethal load Smax = 10⁴ sporangia; transmission goes through a zoospore pool. The IPM is fitted to qPCR loads (ZE per swab; the two load units have no published conversion) and replaces both mechanisms with regressions:

  • Growth. The fitted mean growth (b11 ≈ 0.8 < 1) is not exponential: loads are pulled towards x* = (b01 + b21 T)/(1 − b11), about 9.2 ln ZE (10⁴ ZE) at 20 °C and 1.9 ln ZE at 4 °C. At 20 °C, ln load rises by 0.62 per day from x = 0 (1 ZE), above the 0.035–0.35 per day of sporangia in the Drawert design, and the rate falls to zero at x*.
  • Death. Instead of a threshold, survival declines smoothly with load (the deposited fit): a frog at 10⁴ ZE survives a 3-day step with probability 0.97, and survival is ½ at 13.1 ln ZE (4.7 × 10⁵ ZE), whatever the temperature.
  • Loss of infection has no counterpart in the Briggs model, and transmission is an environmental probability rather than a pool.

test/wilber2016.jl checks that with Briggs-style vital rates (exponential growth at the linearised rate of a Drawert Group A point, death above Smax) the IPM machinery reproduces exponential load growth and the time to reach Smax.
